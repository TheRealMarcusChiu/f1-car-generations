The Shape of SpeedFormula 1, 1950–2026

Six moments when the rules, or one radical car, changed what a Formula 1 car looks like and where its performance comes from: the engine, the floor, the cockpit, the wings.

A note on scope. These are editorially selected turning points, not an official or exhaustive taxonomy of every season or chassis. Each milestone makes only the claims its cited source supports. Photographs are credited individually; most show historic cars at later festivals, shows and museums, and the captions say so.

Six turning points

1950

01 / 06 · 1950

An early championship benchmark

The Alfa Romeo 158 is one representative car from the first World Championship season. It is not offered here as the universal layout of 1950s Formula 1, only as a well-documented reference point.

F1’s own seventy-year comparison describes it as front-engined and rear-wheel drive, powered by a 1.5-litre supercharged inline-eight. Most of the later changes on this page can be measured against that starting point.

Layout
Front engine, rear-wheel drive
Engine
1.5-litre supercharged inline-eight

Source f1-1950-vs-2020 Formula 1: “1950 vs 2020: Cars, drivers, safety and pit stops – how F1 has changed in 70 years”

1977–78

02 / 06 · 1977–78

Downforce from underneath

The Lotus 78 made the ground-effect breakthrough for open-wheel Formula 1 and ushered in that era. Its successor, the Lotus 79, became the first ground-effect car to win the world championship, carrying Mario Andretti to the 1978 title.

The idea, as F1’s technical feature explains it, was to work the air under the car: venturi-shaped underbody sections, sealed along their edges by side skirts, helped pull the car toward the track.

Source f1-lotus-79 Formula 1: “TECH TUESDAY: The Lotus 79, F1’s ground effect marvel”

2014

03 / 06 · 2014

A new hybrid power-unit formula

The 2014 technical regulations introduced a power unit rather than an engine alone: a 1.6-litre V6 turbo combined with two energy-recovery systems, ERS-K and ERS-H.

The same article reports a 100 kg limit on the fuel a car may use in a race.

Power unit
1.6-litre V6 turbo + ERS-K + ERS-H
Race fuel
100 kg limit

Source f1-2014-regs Formula 1: “A racing revolution? Understanding 2014’s technical regulations”

2018

04 / 06 · 2018

Head protection becomes part of the silhouette

All 2018 cars were to be fitted with the Halo. The FIA announcement described its purpose as reducing the risk of head injury from debris or other objects.

It also changed how a Formula 1 car reads at a glance. The hoop over the cockpit is visible on the 2022 and 2026 cars photographed further down this page.

Source f1-halo-2018 Formula 1: “Halo protection system to be introduced for 2018”

Stated purpose

Reduce the risk of head injury from debris or other objects.

2022

05 / 06 · 2022

Underfloor venturi tunnels return to the centre of the design

Under the 2022 rules the floor, shaped with venturi tunnels, sits at the centre of how the cars make downforce. F1’s technical analysis explains why the floor edges carry their ripples and curls: teams shape them to manage the airflow along the sides of the floor.

With the Lotus 79 as the reference point, 2022 opens a new ground-effect era. That is a family resemblance, not a claim that the 2022 cars work the same way mechanically as their 1970s predecessors.

Source f1-2022-floors Formula 1: “TECH TUESDAY: Why ripples and curls adorn the floors of the 2022 cars”

2026

06 / 06 · 2026

Active aero and a new power balance

These are the rules in force for the 2026 season. F1’s guide describes power split roughly half electrical and half internal-combustion, with the cars running on Advanced Sustainable Fuels.

Aerodynamics became active full-time: both wings adjust. On straights they open into a flattened, low-drag mode; in corners they close into a downforce mode. The car’s wings change shape over the course of a lap.

Power
~50% electrical / ~50% combustion
Fuel
Advanced Sustainable Fuels
Aero
Both wings: open on straights, closed in corners

Source f1-2026-guide Formula 1: “The beginner’s guide to the 2026 Formula 1 regulations”
